[Chapter 2](../format/#bits-as-polynomials) protected the format and version information by dividing polynomials whose
coefficients were bits. The data is protected the same way, with one change: the coefficients are bytes. Damage to a
QR code usually comes in patches, such as a smudge or a tear across a cluster of neighbouring modules, and the 8 bits of
each byte usually sit together in a block 2 modules wide and 4 tall ([chapter 6](../placement/) shows the layout). So
the data's error correction works with whole bytes: a damaged byte counts as one error, however many of its bits are
wrong. These are Reed-Solomon codes, the subject of [chapter 5](../reed-solomon/).

Dividing polynomials whose coefficients are bytes takes a way to add, subtract, multiply and divide the bytes
themselves. This chapter builds one.

## From bits to bytes

Chapter 2's arithmetic on bits was ordinary arithmetic with one twist, $1 + 1 = 0$, so that adding was XOR and
subtracting was the same as adding. It followed all the usual rules: the order of a sum or product doesn't matter,
brackets multiply out, and anything except 0 can be divided by, though with only 0 and 1 there's little to divide. A set
of values with arithmetic like this is called a _field_, and a finite one is also called a _Galois field_, after the
mathematician Évariste Galois.

Reed-Solomon codes need a field whose values are the 256 bytes. Encoding needs only adding and multiplying, but
decoding solves equations to find the errors and repair them, and that takes division too.

### Why not ordinary arithmetic?

The obvious choice is ordinary arithmetic, keeping the remainder after dividing by 256, as computers do with 8-bit
numbers. Adding and multiplying work, but dividing doesn't:

- $2 \times 128 = 256$, which leaves a remainder of 0. Two bytes that aren't 0 multiply to 0.
- $2 \times 1 = 2$, and $2 \times 129 = 258$, which also leaves 2. So $2 \div 2$ has two answers, 1 and 129.
- Nothing times 2 leaves 1: twice anything is even, and so is its remainder after dividing by 256. Nothing can be
  divided by 2 reliably, nor by any other even number.

The trouble is that 256 splits into factors, $2 \times 128$. Remainders after dividing by a prime number would work,
but the nearest prime, 257, gives 257 different remainders, one too many for a byte.

### Bytes as polynomials

The way out is chapter 2's idea: read a byte's 8 bits as the coefficients of a polynomial, the first bit for $x^7$ and
the last for $x^0 = 1$:

$$
\texttt{00011101} = x^4 + x^3 + x^2 + 1
$$

Every byte is a polynomial of degree 7 or less with bit coefficients, and every such polynomial is a byte. Adding and
multiplying the polynomials gives a way to add and multiply bytes. There is a catch, and its fix is the one that failed
above, done with a polynomial that can't be split into factors.

## Adding bytes

Adding polynomials adds the coefficients of each power separately. For example, take the bytes 82 and 25:

$$
\begin{aligned}
82 &= \texttt{01010010} = x^6 + x^4 + x \\
25 &= \texttt{00011001} = x^4 + x^3 + 1
\end{aligned}
$$

Both have an $x^4$ term. In ordinary algebra their sum would have $2x^4$, but the coefficients are bits, and as in
chapter 2, $1 + 1 = 0$. So the two $x^4$ terms cancel out:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(x^6 + x^4 + x) + (x^4 + x^3 + 1) \\
&\quad = x^6 + (1 + 1)x^4 + x^3 + x + 1 \\
&\quad = x^6 + x^3 + x + 1
\end{aligned}
$$

Each power is added on its own, and nothing is carried to the next, so lining the bits up under their powers shows that
adding is the XOR of the two bytes:

$$
\begin{array}{r|cccccccc}
& x^7 & x^6 & x^5 & x^4 & x^3 & x^2 & x & 1 \\
\hline
82 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\
25 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\
\hline
75 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 1 & 1
\end{array}
$$

Written as numbers, $82 + 25 = 75$. The sum of two bytes is always a byte. Every byte added to itself gives 0, so each
byte is its own negative, and subtracting is the same as adding.

## Multiplying bytes

Multiplying polynomials multiplies every term of one by every term of the other, and adds up the results. Multiplying
by $x^i$ moves every bit $i$ places to the left, so the product of two bytes $a$ and $b$ is the sum of copies of $a$,
one for each 1 bit in $b$, shifted left by that bit's position. It's long multiplication in binary, but with XOR
instead of carrying. For example, multiplying 3 by itself gives two $x$ terms, which cancel out, as $1 + 1 = 0$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
3 \times 3 &= (x + 1)(x + 1) \\
&= x^2 + x + x + 1 \\
&= x^2 + 1 = 5
\end{aligned}
$$

The product can have up to 15 bits, from $x^{14}$ down to $x^0$, too many for a byte. The fix is the one that failed
for numbers: divide, and keep the remainder. Dividing by a polynomial of degree 8 leaves a remainder of degree 7 or
less, which is a byte. The standard divides by

$$
p(x) = x^8 + x^4 + x^3 + x^2 + 1
$$

or `100011101` in bits (ISO/IEC 18004, 7.5.2). The division is chapter 2's long division: XOR $p(x)$ in under the
leading 1, again and again, until only 8 bits are left.

### Why this polynomial?

Dividing by 256 failed because $256 = 2 \times 128$, so that 2 and 128 multiply to 0. Polynomials can split into factors
too, with the same result. For example, $x^8 + 1 = (x + 1)^8$, so dividing by $x^8 + 1$ instead, the bytes 3
($x + 1$) and 255 ($x^7 + x^6 + \dots + 1$), which multiply to $x^8 + 1$, would give 0.

$p(x)$ can't be split into factors: it is _irreducible_, the polynomial version of a prime number, which is why the
standard calls it the field's "prime modulus polynomial". With it, no two bytes multiply to 0 unless one of them is 0.
There are 30 irreducible polynomials of degree 8, and any of them would give a field of 256 bytes. The next section
shows what makes this one a good choice.

## Powers of 2 and log tables

Multiplying by 2 is the simplest case. 2 is `00000010`, or $x$, so multiplying by 2 shifts the bits one place left. If
a 1 moves out of the byte, the result has degree 8, and one step of division brings it back: XOR with `100011101`. So
the powers of 2 begin as usual, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, but then

$$
\begin{aligned}
2^8 &= \texttt{100000000} \oplus \texttt{100011101} \\
&= \texttt{00011101} = 29
\end{aligned}
$$

and go on 58, 116, 232, 205, 135 and so on. Step through them below, or press Play. The powers of 2 visit every byte
except 0, each exactly once, and arrive back at 1 at $2^{255}$.

So every byte except 0 is a power of 2, and has a _log_: the power $k$, from 0 to 254, for which $2^k$ is that byte.

That isn't so for every irreducible polynomial. The powers of 2 always come back to 1, but they can do it before
reaching every byte. The AES encryption standard uses the irreducible polynomial $x^8 + x^4 + x^3 + x + 1$, and there
the powers of 2 come back to 1 after only 51 steps. A polynomial for which the powers of 2 reach every byte except 0 is
called _primitive_. 16 of the 30 irreducible polynomials of degree 8 are, including the standard's, which is why it can
call 2 the field's _primitive element_ (Annex A).

> **Finding all 16.** A polynomial of degree 8 is $x^8$ plus one of 256 bytes, few enough to try every one. For each,
> start at 1 and keep multiplying by 2, shifting left and XORing with that polynomial, until the powers come back to 1.
> Exactly 16 polynomials take 255 steps, and the standard's is the smallest of them. This finds them without checking
> first which polynomials are irreducible. Under one that splits into factors, some bytes multiply to 0, and no power of
> 2 can be one of them: if $2^k \times b$ were 0, multiplying on by 2 until the powers return to 1 would turn it into
> $b$, but multiplying 0 always gives 0. So the powers can't visit all 255 non-zero bytes.

### Multiplying with logs

Logs turn multiplying into adding, as they do for ordinary numbers:

$$
2^j \times 2^k = 2^{j + k}
$$

The powers repeat every 255 steps, as $2^{255} = 1$, so if $j + k$ is 255 or more, subtract 255. For example,
$3 = 2^{25}$, so $3 \times 3 = 2^{50}$, which is 5, as before.

Software that makes and reads QR codes multiplies this way. A table of the 255 powers and a table of the logs turn every
multiplication into three lookups and an addition, instead of shifting and dividing; the byte multiplier above checks
its answer this way. 0 is the exception: it isn't a power of 2, so it has no log, but anything times 0 is 0 anyway.

## Dividing bytes

The powers of 2 also give every byte except 0 an _inverse_: a byte to multiply it by to get 1. If a byte is $2^k$, its
inverse is $2^{255 - k}$, because

$$
2^k \times 2^{255 - k} = 2^{255} = 1
$$

For example, $2 = 2^1$, so its inverse is $2^{254}$, which is 142. Multiplying 142, `10001110`, by 2 shifts it left to
`100011100`, and XOR with `100011101` leaves `00000001`, or 1. With remainders after dividing by 256, 2 had no inverse
at all. The table of powers above shows the inverse of each byte: point at one to see it.

Dividing by a byte is multiplying by its inverse, which is the same as subtracting logs:

$$
2^j \div 2^k = 2^{j - k}
$$

adding 255 if $j - k$ is negative. Every byte except 0 can be divided by, with exactly one answer.

So bytes can now be added, subtracted, multiplied and divided, following the same rules as ordinary arithmetic. This
is the field the standard calls GF(2⁸): the Galois field with $2^8 = 256$ values (7.5.2).

## Polynomials with byte coefficients

Reed-Solomon codes work with polynomials whose coefficients are bytes. Here $x$ is a new variable, and it no longer
matters that each byte is itself a polynomial: bytes are just values to add and multiply as in this chapter. To keep
the two apart, the standard writes the byte 2 as $\alpha$, so its powers are
$\alpha^0 = 1$, $\alpha^1 = 2$, $\alpha^2 = 4$, up to $\alpha^{254}$ (Annex A).

Adding two polynomials adds the coefficients of each power, with byte addition, XOR. Here $3 + 1 = 2$, $5 + 5 = 0$
and $7 + 2 = 5$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(3x^2 + 5x + 7) &+ (x^2 + 5x + 2) \\
&= 2x^2 + 5
\end{aligned}
$$

Multiplying multiplies every term by every term and adds the results, with byte multiplication. Reed-Solomon codes use
products like this one, where subtracting is adding:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(x - 1)(x - 2) &= x^2 + (1 + 2)x + 1 \times 2 \\
&= x^2 + 3x + 2
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $3 = \alpha^{25}$, the standard writes this as $x^2 + \alpha^{25}x + \alpha$, the first of its _generator
polynomials_ (Table A.1). Chapter 5 shows what they are for.

Dividing is long division again. Each step divides the leading coefficient of what's left by the leading coefficient
of the divisor, and subtracts that multiple of the divisor, which clears the leading term. For example, to divide
$7x^3 + x^2$ by $x^2 + 3x + 2$:

1. The leading term is $7x^3$, so subtract $7x$ times the divisor, $7x^3 + 9x^2 + 14x$. Here $7 \times 3 = 9$: `111`
   times `11` is `111` ⊕ `1110` = `1001`. That leaves $8x^2 + 14x$, as $1 + 9 = 8$.
2. The leading term is now $8x^2$, so subtract 8 times the divisor, $8x^2 + 24x + 16$. That leaves $22x + 16$, as
   $14 + 24 = 22$.

$22x + 16$ has a lower degree than the divisor, so it is the remainder, and the quotient is $7x + 8$. This divisor's
leading coefficient is 1, so each step only needed the leading coefficient of what was left; the divisors are always
like this when encoding, but decoding needs division by other bytes too.

This is a Reed-Solomon encoder in miniature. The dividend is two data bytes, 7 and 1, followed by two zero bytes, and
the divisor is the generator polynomial from above. Putting the remainder in place of the zeros gives
$7x^3 + x^2 + 22x + 16$: the data, then two error correction bytes, 22 and 16, which together make a multiple of
$x^2 + 3x + 2$. [Chapter 5](../reed-solomon/) does the same with real data and longer generators, and shows how a
scanner uses the error correction bytes to find and repair damaged bytes.
